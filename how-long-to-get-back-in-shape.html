Starting again

How long does it take to get back in shape after a break?

Usually less time than you fear. Strength fades slowly during a break, and it comes back faster than it took to build the first time. The first few weeks back are mostly about getting your body used to regular training again.

By Bjørn Lundell, founder of Oreuma · 11 October 2026

What a break actually costs

A few weeks off costs very little strength. Over a longer break you lose some, and muscle size tends to drop a bit faster than strength does. Aerobic fitness, the kind you notice on stairs and hills, usually fades sooner than strength. That's why the first sessions back often feel harder on your breathing than on your muscles.

It comes back faster the second time

Researchers at the University of Jyväskylä in Finland tested this directly. Untrained adults did 20 weeks of strength training: one group trained without a break, the other took 10 weeks off in the middle. The break group lost a little, regained their earlier strength and size within about five weeks of training again, and by the end the two groups had ended up in the same place.

Exactly how this muscle memory works is still being studied. The practical lesson is clear enough: time off sets you back a little, and the way back is shorter than the first climb.

A realistic timeline

  • Weeks 1 to 2: getting used to it. Keep sessions short and easy. Some muscle soreness a day or two after is normal; being wrecked for days means you did too much.
  • Weeks 3 to 4: sessions start to feel familiar. This is when you can add a little: a few more minutes, or a harder version of one movement.
  • Weeks 5 to 8: most people who were active before feel close to their old level. If you're starting from scratch, this is when progress becomes noticeable.

These are rough guides. Age, how long you were away and how active you were before all shift them. The thing that moves the timeline most is regularity: three short sessions a week beat one big one.

The real risk is the first week

Because strength comes back quickly, it's tempting to start where you left off. That's the classic mistake: you feel fine during the session, then you're too sore to move properly for two or three days, and the restart stalls. Starting at about half of what you used to do costs almost nothing and keeps you showing up.

If you have a heart or lung condition, are pregnant or recently gave birth, or are coming back from an illness, injury or surgery, check with your doctor or a physiotherapist before you start. Stop if something hurts sharply, and get help if you feel chest pain, faintness or unusual breathlessness.
