Starting again

Why you keep quitting workout plans, and what to do instead

If you've started and stopped exercising more than once, you're in the majority, and you already have proof of the most useful skill there is: you can start. The fix is a plan that expects gaps instead of breaking on them.

By Bjørn Lundell, founder of Oreuma · 10 October 2026

Stopping is the normal case

Studies of exercise programs have found for decades that around half of the people who start one drop out within the first six months. Work trips, a cold, a new job, a sick child, a stretch of bad weather: life interrupts everyone. People who stay active for years still stop now and then. What they have is an easy way back.

Where most plans go wrong

Most programs are written for an unbroken run of weeks. Week 3 assumes you did weeks 1 and 2. So when you miss a week, you're suddenly behind, and every option feels bad: repeat old weeks, skip ahead to workouts you're not ready for, or start again from the very beginning.

A few other patterns make it worse:

  • Restarting at your old level. Your head remembers what you used to do, so the first session back is far too big. You're sore for days, and the restart stalls before it begins.
  • Streaks and missed-day counters. They feel motivating until the first break. Then the broken number turns a normal gap into a loss, and it's easier not to open the app at all.
  • All-or-nothing sessions. If a workout only counts when it's 45 minutes, a tired Tuesday becomes a skipped Tuesday.

This is a design problem far more than a willpower one, and it can be designed around.

What works better

  1. Plan for the gap. Decide now what you'll do after a missed week: a short, easy session, and no attempt to catch up.
  2. Size each workout to today. How long it's been, how you slept and how you feel should change the session. A smaller session you finish beats a bigger one you dread.
  3. Make the minimum small. Ten minutes counts. On low days, the goal is to keep the thread, and that's enough.
  4. Count returns. Look at how many times you came back this month, rather than how many days in a row you managed.
  5. Raise the difficulty slowly. Let it grow only after several comfortable sessions, so you never have to fight the next one.

If you have a heart or lung condition, are pregnant or recently gave birth, or are coming back from an illness, injury or surgery, check with your doctor or a physiotherapist before you start. Stop if something hurts sharply, and get help if you feel chest pain, faintness or unusual breathlessness.

Why I built Oreuma this way

I kept falling off my own strength training, and coming back too hard was always what finished me off. So Oreuma treats gaps as normal input. It notices how long you've been away and sizes today's session to match, with a gentler session after a longer break. There are no streaks, and your history shows what you did, never what you missed.

If you've been away a while, Oreuma sends at most two gentle reminders, and neither mentions missed days. One of them says it plainly: today's session is easy on purpose.
