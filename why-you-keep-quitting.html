Starting again

Why you keep quitting workout plans, and what to do instead

If you've started and stopped exercising more than once, you're in the majority, and you already have proof of the most useful skill there is: you can start. The fix is to find the moment your restarts usually end and plan around it.

By Bjørn Lundell, founder of Oreuma · 10 October 2026 · Updated 18 October 2026

Stopping is the normal case

Studies of exercise programs have found for decades that around half of the people who start one drop out within the first six months. Work trips, a cold, a new job, a sick child, a stretch of bad weather: life interrupts everyone. People who stay active for years still stop now and then. What they have is an easy way back.

Find your quitting point

Most restarts end at one of a few predictable moments. Read through them and notice which one ended your last attempt. Each has a fix that works with or without an app.

1. You came back too hard

Your head remembers what you used to do, so the first session back is far too big. You're sore for three days, the next workout gets skipped, and the restart stalls in its first week.

The fix: make the first two weeks deliberately small, about half of what you think you can manage, and add more only after a few sessions feel comfortable. Oreuma does this for you: after a month away the session is half the usual volume, and it builds back over seven finished workouts.

2. You missed a week and fell behind

Most programs are written for an unbroken run. Week 3 assumes you did weeks 1 and 2, so a missed week leaves only bad options: repeat old weeks, skip ahead to workouts you're not ready for, or start over.

The fix: decide now what happens after a missed week. One short, easy session, then carry on, with no attempt to catch up. Oreuma has no schedule at all, so there's nothing to fall behind.

3. The streak broke

Streaks feel motivating until the first gap. Then a 40-day count resets to zero, and a normal week off looks like a loss. Psychologists call a related pattern the what-the-hell effect: once a rule is broken, people often drop it completely.

The fix: count returns instead of days in a row. Coming back four times this month is a good month. Oreuma has no streak, and your history shows what you did, never what you missed.

4. The workout didn't fit the day

If a session only counts when it's 45 minutes, a tired Tuesday becomes a skipped Tuesday, and two of those become a stopped habit.

The fix: make the minimum small. Ten minutes counts, and the WHO guidelines agree. Oreuma asks what feels right each day, and its Light option is one short block.

5. You didn't enjoy it

Grinding through movements you dread is a slow way to quit. People tend to stick with exercise they're reasonably good at and like doing.

The fix: drop the exercises you hate and keep the ones you don't mind, even if they look less serious. Oreuma mixes short, playful athletic moves into every session so it isn't only squats and push-ups.

If more than one sounds familiar

Most people recognise two or three. Start with the one that came first in your last restart, because that's usually the one that ended it. For most returners it's the first: coming back too hard.

If you have a heart or lung condition, are pregnant or recently gave birth, or are coming back from an illness, injury or surgery, check with your doctor or a physiotherapist before you start. Stop if something hurts sharply, and get help if you feel chest pain, faintness or unusual breathlessness.
